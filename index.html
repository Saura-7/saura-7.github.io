You run a small cat adoption centre. People bring in cats they found, other people come to adopt, and you decide who gets what.

Game designer and programmer, with two 3D artists. My thesis project, made in Unity.

The hook is attachment. The way a customer looks and behaves should push you to pick the kind option over the profitable one, because you care about that cat.

Four testers filled in my form after playing. It's a small sample, but it told me a lot:

  • 3 of 4 said the cats felt like stock. One said they felt like little characters.
  • 3 of 4 didn't notice the day 1 guide.
  • 3 of 4 turned away someone bringing in a cat, so saying no worked as a real choice.
  • 3 of 4 almost ran out of money, and 3 of 4 said the rent felt fair.

It was a major setback. The cats are the main attraction, and "stock" makes them sound like items from a supermarket instead of actual cats.

Players will name each cat themselves, and each cat will behave according to its trait: angry, skittish, playful or lazy. Cat preferences and customer dialogue come later. The next build is due at the end of November, and I'll test whether "stock" turns into "characters".

Reputation used to go up when you accepted a deal and down when you rejected one, which made accepting everything the right answer. Now it comes from how well an adopted cat was cared for, and saying no is free.

2000s rural India. Abhinav faints in a rotting, dusty house and wakes to find it unnaturally clean. Now he has to get out.

Game designer and programmer, with two artists. Made in 2 to 3 months in Unity.

I wanted the old feeling of being a kid told not to play outside, back when there wasn't much security around. That sets the mood before anything strange happens.

  • A village outside the house. It diluted the claustrophobia and stretched the schedule.
  • A visible character. Seeing a model detached players from the fear, so it became first person.
  • A deep inventory. You carry one item at a time, so choosing between crowbar and key creates natural backtracking.

First you find a crowbar, because planks block the way. Then you break open doors, read the lore and find codes. Finally you use a key, pull a lever and find the hatch.

I loved seeing people spot the small details we added, and how immersed they got. Testing also showed that some textures broke the mood, so I reworked them so colours shift from warm tones to sickly greens as you go deeper.

Two kings on a grid board. Move one tile at a time like a chess king, open chests for coins and power-ups, and build towers to destroy the other kingdom.

Designer and junior developer, with a lead programmer and an artist. It started as a paper prototype.

The randomness of chest spawning and the economic balance became obvious problems the moment the paper prototype came to life. I had to study game math and formulas to balance the economy.

Chests come in five rarities, from wood to diamond, and each has its own zone on the board. I wanted them to stay random but follow a simple pattern, with an underlying algorithm deciding how random they could be.

It's the combination of chess king movement and the strategy of tower defense.

A paused horror project about following a daily routine and noticing what's slowly wrong with it.

Five days, one routine: wake up, make coffee, work, do a chore, eat, sleep. Because you repeat it, you learn what normal looks like. Then a closet door is slightly open, or the cabinets aren't fully shut, and you notice.

I built this under a 3-hour limit, including the documentation. The core is an insanity system tied to hunger and thirst: look after yourself badly and you start seeing things. Notes left by earlier visitors lead you from place to place.
